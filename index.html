02 The starting advantage

The hardest part takes years.

The software that keeps an industry running rarely becomes a household name. But its founders have earned something far more valuable than attention: customers who trust them, a place in everyday workflows, and firsthand knowledge of how the market works. That’s where we begin.

03 Why these markets

We choose where to compete.

Some of the best software companies serve industries most people never think about. They can be profitable, deeply useful, and surprisingly hard to replace. Yet their markets may attract little venture capital, and their size can put them below the threshold of larger buyers.

The industries we pursue are rarely fashionable. That’s part of the appeal.

III

Expand the work

Software often sits in the middle of a much larger job. Carefully controlled agents can help complete parts of that job, from preparing information to coordinating defined actions, with permissions, evaluation, and a person accountable.

L1
Task efficiency

Automate the lookup. They still step out, just for less time.

Measure: hours and cost per transaction

L2
Behavioral change

A trustworthy answer inside the moment. They stay with the customer.

Measure: turnaround, completion and consistency

L3
Economic expansion

Customers nobody had time for make this week’s list, with a reason and a next step.

Measure: coverage, recovery and incremental revenue, against a baseline that can fail

L1 saves labor. L2 changes the pattern. L3 creates activity that wasn’t happening. We claim the level we can evidence.

These are complementary opportunities, not a standard checklist applied to every company. Customer need and actual economics determine the order.

05 Sound businesses first

The business has to work before the thesis does.

We begin with durable software economics. We don’t count on speculative agent revenue to justify an acquisition.

Over time, we see opportunities to deliver more: useful intelligence, completed tasks, new services and, in select cases, measurable outcomes. Each has to earn its way through customer validation, reliable execution, and real unit economics.

07 For founders

Worthy of what you’ve built.

You spent years earning customer trust, building a product people rely on, and learning a market few outsiders understand. If you’re thinking about your next chapter, you deserve more than a flattering pitch.

We intend to build, and that comes with change. We won’t pretend otherwise. What we offer is serious attention to the customers and team who made the business valuable, and accountability for the decisions we make.

We generally invite founders to retain a stake and a voice, with the specifics agreed and documented in each transaction.

The questions we’d ask in your shoes.

Straight answers
01What will happen to my team?
We take people seriously and set clear expectations. Some responsibilities and structures may change. We don’t offer blanket job guarantees; we aim to build a capable organization with meaningful work, honest feedback, and fair incentives.
02Will you change the product?
We intend to invest in it. Customers’ reliability and continuity come first. Beyond that, we build based on real needs and evidence, not technology theater.
03Will I still have a say?
We generally seek an ongoing strategic connection. The actual scope, governance, time commitment, and financial terms are agreed as part of the deal.
04What have you already proved?
We can talk concretely about Pixifi and SuperCat. The broader agentic and cross-company model is still being developed. We’ll distinguish demonstrated performance from ambition.
05What if the ambitious plan doesn’t work?
We buy businesses that make sense on existing software economics, independent of future agent revenue. Changes still carry risk. We’ll discuss the operating plan, financing, oversight, and contingencies in private diligence.

Not a solicitation to sell, a representation of guaranteed deal terms, or a commitment to a specific outcome.
